Chapter 1: Meet the twins

I was built with a twin sister, Spirit. We're about the size of a golf cart, with six wheels and solar panels for power. A girl named Sofi Collis, only nine years old, wrote the essay that gave us our names.

Science: how I was built

Opportunity was one of two Mars Exploration Rovers. She launched in July 2003 and carried cameras, a rock-grinding tool and instruments that measure what rocks are made of. Her job was to act as a robot geologist.

Chapter 2: Landing day

I didn't land gently. Airbags wrapped around me, and I bounced across Mars like a beach ball before I stopped. When the bags deflated, I was inside a small crater called Eagle Crater. The team called it a hole-in-one.

Science: why this spot?

Orbiting spacecraft had found the mineral hematite at Meridiani Planum. On Earth, hematite often forms where water was present. The team sent Opportunity there to test whether Mars was once wet.

Chapter 3: The blueberries

Right away I noticed tiny round pebbles scattered everywhere. The team nicknamed them blueberries. Something made them. Help me work out what.

Science: what the clues mean

The blueberries are hematite spherules. The rocks were rich in sulfate minerals, and rock layers showed ripple patterns. Together these gave strong evidence that liquid water once soaked and flowed through this area of Mars.

Chapter 4: 90 days became 14 years

My mission was planned for 90 sols, which are Martian days. I'm still proud that I kept going far longer than anyone expected.

Science: how did I last so long?

A sol is about 24 hours and 40 minutes. Wind gusts and dust devils sometimes blew dust off the solar panels, which gave the rover a power boost. Careful planning by the engineers also helped.

Science: clays and a visitor from space

At Endeavour Crater, Opportunity found clay minerals, which form in gentler water than the acidic water suggested by the sulfates. She also found Heat Shield Rock, the first meteorite identified on another planet.

Chapter 6: Staying alive

Mars is hard on machines. Winter nights are freezing and the sunlight gets weak. My engineers on Earth had to keep solving problems for me.

Winter is coming and my solar panels are making less power. What should the team do?

Science: real fixes

The team parked Opportunity on north-facing slopes in winter so her panels caught more sunlight. When a front wheel stopped steering properly, they drove her backward for years. They also changed how she used her memory when it began to fail.

Chapter 7: The storm and the goodbye

In 2018, a dust storm grew until it covered the whole planet. The sky went dark, and my panels couldn't get enough light. The team sent me wake-up messages again and again. I never answered.

Science: how the mission ended

Opportunity's last contact with Earth was in June 2018. After many attempts to reach her, NASA declared the mission complete in February 2019. You may have seen the line "My battery is low and it's getting dark". It was a poetic paraphrase by a journalist, not a real transmission.

What I made possible

I found out that Mars once had water. That helped scientists choose where Curiosity and Perseverance should go, and it keeps the dream of bringing Mars rocks to Earth alive. I was abandoned, but never forgotten.

Glossary
Sol
One Martian day, about 24 hours and 40 minutes.
Hematite
An iron mineral that often forms in water on Earth.
Spectrometer
A tool that finds out what something is made of by measuring light or particles.
Meteorite
A rock from space that lands on a planet.
